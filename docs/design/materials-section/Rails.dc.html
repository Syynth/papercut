How a ramp's rail tiles, with end caps
Proposed. A rail is tagged the way a fringe already is: three tiles, told apart by which of their two top corners carry the rail slot. The mesher walks the ramp's open side in half-tile pieces and picks each piece's tile by whether the rail carries on past that point, so a run of any length gets a cap at its head and a cap at its foot. Seen here from the side.

The three tiles

Start cap[ –, rail, –, – ]The rail begins here and carries on to the right: the newel at the head of the ramp.
Middle[ rail, rail, –, – ]Rail on both sides. Its art repeats at the half tile, so either half can follow either.
End cap[ rail, –, –, – ]The rail comes in from the left and stops: the newel at the foot.
The same three shapes a fringe has today (straight, runs in from the left, runs in from the right). Each tile is a whole tile tall; how tall the rail stands is whatever the art draws, so a kerb is low and a banister is high with no setting for it.

Sheared — the strip runs parallel to the slope

Three pieces to a cell, like the ramp's surface: the slope is a tile and a half long, and each piece is half a tile of art laid along it. The extra middle piece comes from the middle tile. Posts lean with the slope, which suits kerbs, ropes and low walls.

Upright — the art stands straight and steps down

Two pieces to a cell, because an upright rail is as long as the ramp's footprint, not its slope: no squeeze. Each piece drops half a tile from the last, which is one step of a stair drawn at the half tile. Posts stay vertical, which suits banisters.

To decide. Caps at both ends for both styles, as drawn? On a single cell the sheared rail is cap, middle, cap and the upright one is cap against cap. Should a rail that reaches a plateau with a fence or fringe on it still cap, or carry on into it? And does the rail stand on the ramp's edge, or out from it by a distance per material, the way a picket does?
