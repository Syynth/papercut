How a ramp's rail tiles, with end caps
Decided 2026-09-19. A rail is tagged as a patch seen from the side: the corners below its top edge carry the rail slot, so its tiles are the arrangements a material already has. The mesher walks the ramp's open side in half-tile pieces and picks each piece's tile by whether the rail carries on past that point, so a run of any length gets a cap at its head and a cap at its foot. Seen here from the side.

The tiles

End cap[ –, –, rail, – ]The rail comes in from the left and stops: the newel at the foot.
Body[ rail, rail, rail, rail ]What the rail is made of below its top edge. Repeats down to the ground, as many rows as the ramp is high. Optional: a rail with no body is one row, as before.
Landings. Upright rails only. A setting on the material, Landings: off / on, runs the rail half a tile onto the level ground at its head and foot. A second slot, landing, tags that art: the caps carry it, and two bends, [ –, –, landing, rail ] and [ –, –, rail, landing ], join it to the rail. Where the ground there is taken or not level, the rail caps at the ramp. A sloped rail always caps at the ramp.
Left and right edges finish a body's ends. Place block writes them all at once.

Sloped — was “sheared”: the strip is turned to lie along the slope, its posts square to it, never skewed

Three pieces to a cell, like the ramp's surface: the slope is a tile and a half long, and each piece is half a tile of art laid along it. The extra middle piece comes from the middle tile. The art is turned, not skewed: every piece stays a rectangle, so posts stand square to the slope and keep their drawn thickness. Along its length it is drawn a touch short (the true slope is 1.41 tiles, the art 1.5). Suits kerbs, ropes and low walls.
The ramp's side. The triangle under the slope, gridded above, is a face of its own: a side slot of the ramp. It is tiled like a wall, with corner tags on the half-tile grid so it blends with the wall beside it and the material under it, and the mesher cuts each tile along the slope. With no side art it takes the material's wall art. Direction applies, so art drawn running east is mirrored for west. A sloped rail sits above the cut; an upright rail's body covers it.

Upright — the art stands straight and steps down

Two columns to a cell, because an upright rail is as long as the ramp's footprint, not its slope: no squeeze. Each column stands on the high side of its half tile and drops half a tile from the last, so the rail's foot is a staircase that overlaps the slope. Below the top row the body repeats in half-tile pieces down to the ground, covering the ramp's triangular side: a stringer under a banister. Everything stays on the half-tile grid, so nothing is cut.

Still open. Decided: caps at both ends for both styles, the side slot, the body to the ground. On a single cell the sloped rail is cap, middle, cap and the upright one is cap against cap. Landings are an upright material's setting, off by default. Should a rail that reaches a plateau with a fence or fringe on it still cap, or carry on into it? Is a landing always half a tile, or as long as the material says? Is the ramp's side a slot of its own, cut along the slope as drawn, and does an upright rail's body run all the way to the ground? And does the rail stand on the ramp's edge, or out from it by a distance per material, the way a picket does?
